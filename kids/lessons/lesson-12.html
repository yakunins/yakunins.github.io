Theory

What Is Phishing?

Phishing is a type of cybercrime in which an attacker impersonates a trusted organisation — a bank, a social network, a delivery service — to trick you into handing over sensitive information such as passwords, credit card numbers, or personal details. The name is a play on “fishing”: attackers cast a lure and hope someone bites.

Phishing comes in several flavours. The most common is email phishing, where a mass-sent message pretends to be from a well-known brand. Smishing does the same thing through text messages (“SMS phishing”). Vishing uses phone calls. And website clones replicate the look of a real login page — pixel for pixel — so you enter your credentials without suspecting anything.

Spear phishing is a more targeted version: instead of sending the same email to millions, the attacker researches a specific victim and crafts a personalised message. When the target is a senior executive, this is called whaling.

Social Engineering: Manipulating Humans, Not Computers

Social engineering is a broader category that includes phishing. Instead of breaking through firewalls or cracking encryption, social engineers exploit the weakest link in any security system: people. They manipulate human psychology — trust, helpfulness, fear, curiosity — to get victims to do what the attacker wants.

The legendary hacker Kevin Mitnick once said that it was easier to trick someone into giving him a password than to spend the effort to hack into a system. His book, The Art of Deception, is filled with real-world examples of social engineering attacks that succeeded simply because someone on the phone was too polite to say “no.”

Common techniques include pretexting (inventing a believable story to gain trust), baiting (leaving an infected USB drive where someone will pick it up), and tailgating (following an authorised person through a locked door).

Red Flags: How to Spot a Scam

Most phishing attempts share a set of telltale warning signs. Learning to spot them is your best defence:

  • Urgency — “Your account will be closed in 24 hours!” Scammers create panic so you act before you think.
  • Too good to be true — “You’ve won a free iPhone!” If it sounds unbelievable, it probably is.
  • Sender mismatch — the display name says “Apple Support,” but the actual email address is apple-supp0rt@random-domain.xyz.
  • Suspicious links — hover over a link (without clicking!) to see the real URL. Look for misspellings like paypa1.com instead of paypal.com.
  • Generic greetings — “Dear Customer” instead of your actual name.
  • Spelling and grammar errors — legitimate companies proofread their emails.
  • Unexpected attachments — a “receipt” or “invoice” you never asked for may contain malware.

What to Do If You Suspect a Scam

If an email, text, or phone call feels off, follow these steps:

  1. Do not click any links or download any attachments.
  2. Do not reply or call back the number provided in the message. If the message claims to be from your bank, look up the bank’s real phone number on their official website and call that instead.
  3. Check the sender’s actual email address or phone number — not just the display name.
  4. Report the message. Most email providers have a “Report phishing” button. You can also forward suspicious emails to the Anti-Phishing Working Group at reportphishing@apwg.org.
  5. If you already clicked a link or entered your password, change that password immediately and enable two-factor authentication on the affected account.
  6. Tell a trusted adult. Reporting scams is not embarrassing — it helps protect everyone around you.

Remember: legitimate organisations will never ask you to share your password via email or text. If in doubt, go directly to the official website by typing the URL into your browser instead of clicking any link.

Practice Task

Build: “Phishing Detective”

Create an HTML/CSS/JS page that tests whether users can tell real messages from phishing scams:

  1. Display 10 short descriptions of emails or websites (a mix of legitimate and phishing). Show each one in a card with a sender name, subject line, and a brief preview of the message body.
  2. Under each card, place two buttons: “Legit” and “Phishing.”
  3. After the user picks an answer for every card, show their score out of 10.
  4. Below the score, reveal the correct answer for each card along with a short explanation of which red flags gave it away (or why it was legitimate).

Example cards to include:

  • A fake “Your Apple ID has been locked” email from apple-security@mail-verify.xyz (phishing — domain mismatch, urgency)
  • A real order confirmation from auto-confirm@amazon.com for an item you actually bought (legit)
  • A text saying “You’ve won a $1,000 gift card! Claim now!” from an unknown number (phishing — too good to be true)
  • A school newsletter from your actual school’s domain (legit)
  • A “Verify your bank account” email with spelling errors and a generic greeting (phishing)
  • A real password-reset email you requested from noreply@github.com (legit)
  • A “Package delivery failed” SMS with a shortened bit.ly link (phishing — unexpected, suspicious link)
  • A friend’s real message on a platform you both use (legit)
  • A “Google security alert” from no-reply@accounts.google.com confirming a new sign-in you just made (legit)
  • An email from “PayPal” sent from paypa1-support@secure-check.net asking you to confirm a transaction (phishing — misspelled name, wrong domain)

Requirements:

  • Each card styled to look like a mini email preview (sender, subject, snippet)
  • “Legit” and “Phishing” buttons per card, greyed out after clicking
  • A “Show Results” button that only appears once all 10 are answered
  • Correct answers shown in green, wrong answers in red, with explanations
  • A final score with a short message (“You’re a Phishing Detective!” for 9–10, encouragement for lower scores)
