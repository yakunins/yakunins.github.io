Theory

Everything You’ve Learned — the Big Picture

Over the past nineteen lessons, you built an understanding of the Internet from the ground up. In Module 1 you discovered how packets travel across routers, how IP addresses and the Domain Name System give every device a reachable identity, and how protocols like TCP and UDP keep data flowing.

Module 2 took you inside the World Wide Web — HTML for structure, CSS for style, JavaScript for interactivity, and HTTP as the language browsers and servers speak. In Module 3 you confronted the risks — phishing, malware, identity theft, and cyberbullying — and learned to defend yourself with encryption, two-factor authentication, and sharp critical thinking. Module 4 explored advanced topics like APIs, cloud computing, and responsive design. And Module 5 looked ahead — artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things, net neutrality, and your role in shaping the digital future.

That is a lot of ground. This final lesson ties it all together into something practical — a personal action plan you can follow starting today.

Building Your Personal Security Checklist

Knowing about threats is useful, but only if you act on that knowledge. A security checklist turns abstract ideas into concrete habits. Here is a starter list you can adapt:

  • Run a password audit — replace weak or reused passwords with unique ones at least 16 characters long, generated by a password manager.
  • Enable two-factor authentication on every account that supports it — email, social media, gaming platforms.
  • Review privacy settings on each social-media account. Decide who can see your posts, your friends list, and your location.
  • Check app permissions on your phone. Does that flashlight app really need access to your contacts?
  • Keep your operating system, browser, and apps up to date — updates often patch security holes.
  • Back up important files to an external drive or a trusted cloud service on a regular schedule.

Treat this checklist like a recurring appointment — revisit it every few months, because new accounts appear, old passwords linger, and privacy policies change without warning.

How to Keep Learning — Trusted Resources

Technology moves fast. The concepts you learned in this course will stay relevant, but the specific tools and threats will evolve. Here are places to keep your knowledge fresh:

  • Khan Academy — Internet 101 — free video lessons covering networking, encryption, and cybersecurity.
  • Common Sense Education — age-appropriate digital citizenship curriculum with lesson plans and discussion guides.
  • Electronic Frontier Foundation — the EFF publishes guides on surveillance self-defence, privacy tools, and digital rights.
  • CISA — Secure Our World — the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers plain-language tips for families.
  • MDN Web Docs — the go-to reference when you want to dig deeper into HTML, CSS, and JavaScript.
  • Wayback Machine — the Internet Archive lets you explore how websites have changed over time.

Bookmark these resources. When a new headline about a data breach or a privacy scandal appears, you will have the vocabulary and the instinct to understand what happened and what to do about it.

The Internet Is a Tool — You Decide How to Use It

The Internet is not inherently good or bad. It is a network of networks — cables, routers, protocols, and software — that amplifies whatever humans put into it. The same infrastructure that enables open-source collaboration, free education, and global communication also enables scams, misinformation, and harassment.

Your generation has grown up online. That means you understand the digital world in ways previous generations never did. You know how algorithms shape the content you see. You know the difference between a legitimate link and a phishing attempt. You know that a “free” app often means your personal data is the real product.

Use that knowledge. Build things — websites, apps, communities. Question things — terms of service, default settings, trending narratives. Protect yourself and help others do the same. The Internet belongs to everyone who uses it, and its future depends on people who understand how it works and care about making it better.

Congratulations on finishing this course. The real lesson starts now — every time you go online.

Final Project

Build: “Personal Internet Safety Dashboard”

This is your capstone project — a multi-section, single-page web application that brings together everything you’ve learned across all twenty lessons. You will combine HTML, CSS, and JavaScript to build a functional, interactive dashboard with five sections:

Section 1 — Home

A welcome page that greets the user by name (stored in localStorage after a first-visit prompt) and displays a short summary of what this course covered. Include the five module names as a visual timeline or card layout.

Section 2 — Safety Checklist

An interactive checklist of personal security actions. Each item should have a checkbox. When the user checks an item, save the state to localStorage so progress persists across page reloads. Include at least these items:

  • Run a password audit — replace weak or reused passwords
  • Set up two-factor authentication on all accounts
  • Review privacy settings on every social-media profile
  • Check app permissions on your phone
  • Update your operating system and browser
  • Create a backup of important files
  • Check if your email has appeared in a data breach

Display a progress bar or percentage showing how many items are complete.

Section 3 — Scam Spotter

A mini phishing quiz. Show 4–5 screenshots or text descriptions of emails, messages, or websites and ask the user to decide: “Legit or scam?” Track the score and display a result at the end. Use JavaScript to randomize the order each time.

Section 4 — Resources

A curated list of links to trusted cybersecurity resources for teens. Organize them by category (learning, tools, news). Include at least:

  • Khan Academy Internet 101
  • Common Sense Digital Citizenship
  • EFF Security Resources
  • CISA Secure Our World
  • MDN Web Docs

Section 5 — About

A short section describing what you learned in this course. Write it in your own words — what surprised you, what changed how you think about the Internet, and what you plan to do differently going forward. This is your personal reflection.

Technical Requirements

  • Use CSS navigation (tabs, sidebar, or scroll-linked sections) to switch between the five sections without reloading the page
  • Store the user’s name and checklist progress in localStorage
  • Use JavaScript addEventListener for all interactivity — no inline onclick
  • Make the layout responsive — it should work on both desktop and mobile screens
  • Use CSS custom properties (variables) for your colour scheme
  • Write clean, commented code — someone reading your source should understand your thinking
